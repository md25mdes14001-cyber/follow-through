Models and strategies: what EcoCraft Phase 2 borrows, and what is different

Status: DONE (desk research, 16 November 2026). Everything said here about EcoCraft Phase 2 is PLANNED. Nothing reports a result. Pages attempted for this file: 34 (24 opened, 10 failed). Sources reused from earlier verified files are cited by their IDs (B6–B13 in GLOBAL_BENCHMARKS.md; #22 in sources/literature_verified.md; F-rows in PARTNER_MAP.md). Every number below comes from an opened page, with its URL, or carries [UNVERIFIED]. "Borrows", "fit" and "does not transfer" are analysis, not claims made by the sources.

If you read only this

  1. Nineteen models were read on opened pages: nine Indian and ten international. The Indian ones that kept people segregating (Indore, Ambikapur, Kerala's Haritha Karma Sena, SWaCH in Pune, Hasiru Dala in Bengaluru) share one base: a known person collects at a known time, is paid from fees and material sales, and has somewhere to send the material.
  2. Where that operating layer is missing, built assets stand idle. A 2024 report from rural Madhya Pradesh found village sheds and recovery facilities shut or unused (I8).
  3. Low-value plastic is the weak link everywhere. It made up about 82% of the dry waste collected door to door in Bengaluru (I5); Kerala panchayats pay ₹7–10 per kg to get rid of it (I3); Mysuru moved it only once brands' EPR money paid for a route (I6).
  4. EcoCraft Phase 2 (PLANNED) borrows that operating base and adds three things that none of the 19 models states it does in full: a per-batch Trust Receipt to the people who handed the waste over, a Claim Ladder rule that stops every claim at the last evidenced rung, and a contractor's worksite as the temporary host for nearby villages.
  5. Ecobricks stay as the hook and a visible proof object, packed to the published standard and labelled as storage, not recycling.
  6. Section 4 sets out eight strategies, each with its measure. Section 5 names the variables and the pilot design, matched to hypotheses H1–H5 in THESIS_DOSSIER.md.

1. What this project targets: a known receiver, repeat participation and claims that hold

Who Phase 2 works with (PLANNED, 8–10 weeks): a workmen colony at the L&T Satna MVRWSS site, women's groups in nearby villages, and 2–3 nearby schools, building on Phase 1 (DONE: awareness sessions, 10–15 ecobricks, own count; see 01_Fixed/EcoCraft/SATNA_FACT_SHEET.md). The matched-group comparison in the dossier covers 8 of those weeks. L&T has heard a phased proposal; no decision is recorded.

No baselines exist yet. Weeks 1–2 of Phase 2 set them. So most targets below are relative (against the group's own baseline or the control group), and the few fixed numbers are either rules or taken from a published standard.

# Target (all PLANNED) How it is measured Proposed level, and why
T1 Repeat participation: people keep handing over after week 1 Returning ÷ total present, rolling four weeks, by head-count with no names (dossier §8) Proposed target, to be agreed: Trust Receipt groups stay above control groups by more than the week-to-week noise seen at baseline. This is H1's own test. No absolute number is set because there is no baseline; the Delhi study saw segregation fall from 54% to 43% in five weeks without feedback (Wadehra & Mishra, 2018, in statistics_verified.md #9), so decay is the expected pattern to beat (H3).
T2 Clean, segregated dry plastic Contamination, % by weight, in a weekly sample per group (dossier §8) Proposed target, to be agreed with the receiver in week 1: at or below the receiver's acceptance limit by week 4. The receiver sets the limit, and it is not yet known. For ecobricks the standard is published: density above 0.33 g/ml and no more than 0.70 g/ml, clean and dry plastic only (N1, ecobricks.org).
T3 A known receiver for every batch Share of batch weight delivered with a slip naming receiver, date and weight (Claim Ladder rung 3) Proposed target, to be agreed: 100% of batches either delivered with a slip or reported as "not delivered". The figure is a rule, not an aspiration: a batch without a slip cannot be reported above rung 2.
T4 Feedback actually reaches people Fidelity log: did each Trust Receipt group get its receipt, on paper or WhatsApp, within 7 days of its handover? Proposed target, to be agreed: every week of the comparison (8 of 8). If receipts are late or skipped, H1 cannot be tested. Seven days keeps the receipt inside the weekly cycle, before the next handover.
T5 Honest reporting Count of public statements (receipts, slides, posts, reports) that claim a rung above the last evidenced one Rule: zero. Every claim names its rung (see CLAIM_LADDER.md).
T6 Community ownership by women's groups and schools Weeks in which a local woman or teacher runs the handover and the Seva Circle without the researcher present Proposed target, to be agreed with the groups: from week 6, at least half of the remaining weeks. The worksite host is temporary, so the test is whether the routine survives the researcher's absence.
T7 Collector safety and fair pay Sharp items handed over unwrapped, per week; collector hours paid (dossier §8–9) Rule: no unpaid collector hours. Sharp items: a falling trend from the group's own baseline; no number, because no baseline exists.
T8 No harm to existing livelihoods Kilograms of saleable material (PET, rigid plastic, metal) that still go to local kabadiwalas, per week Rule: saleable material keeps going to existing buyers (dossier §9). Phase 2 handles the low-value fraction they do not buy.
T9 A cost a CSR team can budget Cost per kg each week: bags, transport, collector time Reported weekly with its parts. No target level: the aim is a known number, not a low one.

2. Indian models: regular service by paid local workers made segregation stick; low-value plastic is still unsolved

Nine models, chosen for relevance to household waste, women's groups, schools, small towns or villages, and low-value plastic. Alappuzha (Kerala) was opened but left out: the page described wet-waste treatment and said nothing about dry or plastic waste (see Sources).

I1 · Indore, Madhya Pradesh: service first, then segregation, then refusal of mixed waste
What it is Indore Municipal Corporation's door-to-door collection, from a two-ward pilot in 2015 to full coverage by the end of 2016. Households segregate; staff refuse waste that is not segregated.
Key strategy Make collection daily and reliable first, then ask for segregation, then refuse mixed waste.
Evidence ThePrint (27 Nov 2021): categories grew from two to six; user charges ₹60 (slums), ₹90 (middle-class areas), ₹150 (posh areas) a month, with 86% recovery; about 1,500 vehicles and 11,000 sanitation workers, most of them women; officials said residents accepted segregation once vans came daily; NEPRA's plant processes 400 t of dry waste a day under a 14-year agreement from 2018. The Quint, from IndiaSpend (2 Oct 2019): vans play a song each morning and residents come out when they hear it; collection times were moved to suit residents' routines; NGOs, religious leaders' sermons and school competitions and oaths carried the message; repeat offenders were fined; a helpline had a 48-hour resolution rule; 468 vans tracked from a command centre; ₹27 crore user fees in 2017-18. Caveats: figures come from officials, contractors and a van driver; the "95% segregation" figure is a driver's remark and is not used (see statistics_verified.md #7); sanitation spending is large (about ₹1,200 crore a year, ThePrint).
Fit to Satna Partial. Same state, so the name carries weight with MP officials. But Indore is a city of over 30 lakh with a municipal budget and powers to fine; Satna Phase 2 is a CSR pilot around a rural worksite.
Phase 2 borrows A fixed weekly handover with a recognisable call, timed to each group's routine; add categories only after the first one works.
Does not transfer Fines and refusal backed by municipal power. A CSR pilot cannot fine anyone, and refusing a low-value bag that has no buyer pushes it towards burning.
Sources https://theprint.in/india/these-are-the-secrets-to-indores-5-year-cleanest-city-streak-and-its-not-rocket-science/772362 ; https://www.thequint.com/news/india/how-indore-set-an-example-by-becoming-the-cleanest-city-in-2019 (both accessed 2026-11-16). Also PARTNER_MAP.md F6.

I2 · Ambikapur, Chhattisgarh: a women's cooperative runs collection and sorting, paid from fees and sales
What it is Women's self-help groups, federated as the Swachh Ambikapur Mission Sahakari Samiti, collect door to door every day and sort at Solid Liquid Resource Management (SLRM) centres, under the Municipal Corporation.
Key strategy A women's cooperative runs collection and sorting, paid from user fees plus material sales.
Evidence Mongabay-India (30 May 2023): 470 women in 34 SHGs; 19 SLRM centres covering 48 wards; three-member teams each serve 200–250 houses; about 50 t collected a day, about 90% sorted the same day into over 100 subcategories and sold, about 10% sent to cement factories as fuel; scrap dealers buy each evening for cash; fees ₹50 a month for households and ₹100–5,000 for businesses; fines ₹100 (first) and ₹500 (repeat) for not segregating; 2022-23 income almost ₹35 million (user charges ₹18.3 m, dry waste ₹12.5 m, compost ₹4 m); pay about ₹9,000 per member a month since March 2023, up from ₹2,000–2,500 early on. Caveats on the page: workers often skip gloves and masks; the CCTV system no longer works; anti-littering enforcement is weak; 5–10% of houses still hand over mixed waste; an auto-rickshaw driver said the corporation acts mainly near the national cleanliness survey. Note: 470 women at ₹9,000 a month is more than the stated income; the article says part of a sanitation tax is included. The Indian Tribal (2 Jan 2022): a founder member, a tribal woman, says sanitation workers used to be treated as untouchable and residents now call them "Swachchta Didi" and respect the work.
Fit to Satna Strong on the operator model (women's groups, small city of about 2 lakh people). Weak on scale: Phase 2 covers one colony and a few villages, with no fee base.
Phase 2 borrows A named women's team runs the handover point, keeps income from saleable plastic, and is introduced to contributors by name.
Does not transfer User fees and fines need a municipal mandate; 100+ subcategories need daily volume and a centre.
Sources https://india.mongabay.com/2023/05/ambikapurs-women-led-waste-management-system-also-generates-revenue-for-the-city/ ; https://theindiantribal.com/2022/01/02/swachchta-didi (both accessed 2026-11-16).

I3 · Kerala, Haritha Karma Sena: women's teams collect dry waste on fixed dates for a flat fee
What it is Teams of Kudumbashree women, selected by each local body, collect non-biodegradable waste door to door against a user fee. Material goes through ward-level mini Material Collection Facilities to panchayat facilities, and Clean Kerala Company aggregates it.
Key strategy Women's teams collect dry waste on fixed dates for a flat fee, through local collection points.
Evidence Kudumbashree page: each worker visits at least 250 houses; waste is sorted by type (PVC, steel, bottles, e-waste and others); shredded plastic goes to road tarring through Clean Kerala Company; partners are Haritha Keralam Mission, Suchitwa Mission and Clean Kerala Company. Onmanorama (21 Jan 2023): 30,890 members statewide; ₹50–100 a month per household; a proposal to make the fee compulsory and require a fee receipt or card for local-body services; collection on set dates each month. Onmanorama (13 Aug 2024): the minister planned weekly removal of plastic. Onmanorama (18 Sep 2026), Kollam district: 3,026 members in 74 consortia across 73 local bodies (68 of them grama panchayats); ₹50 a month for households, ₹100 for businesses; 2,797 t of inorganic waste handled; panchayats pay agencies ₹7–10 per kg to dispose of non-recyclable or low-value plastic; a plan (not yet in force) to pay households for segregating. The same article reports over ₹29 crore "generated" in 2025-26 with no breakdown; that works out to over ₹100 per kg handled, which the article does not explain, so the figure is not used.
Fit to Satna Strong on rural reach (grama panchayats), women's groups and fixed dates. Weak on the state machinery behind it (a statewide mission, a state aggregator, fee enforcement).
Phase 2 borrows A fixed-date dry-waste round run by a women's team, with a written slip at each handover.
Does not transfer Compulsory fees enforced through local-body services. Its paid disposal of low-value plastic shows Phase 2 must budget for moving it.
Sources https://www.kudumbashree.org/pages/677 ; https://www.onmanorama.com/news/kerala/2023/01/21/user-fee-for-plastic-waste-collection-to-be-made-mandatory-soon.html ; https://www.onmanorama.com/news/kerala/2024/08/13/kerala-government-collect-plastic-waste-households-weekly-mb-rajesh.html ; https://www.onmanorama.com/news/kerala/2026/09/18/kollam-waste-management-success-haritha-karmasena-incentives.amp.html (all accessed 2026-11-16).

I4 · SWaCH, Pune: existing waste pickers become the paid door-to-door service
What it is A cooperative of self-employed waste pickers that collects door to door under an agreement with Pune Municipal Corporation. It grew out of the KKPKP waste pickers' union (1993).
Key strategy Integrate existing waste pickers as the paid door-to-door service instead of bypassing them.
Evidence swachcoop.com ("Impact 2025", self-reported): 10 lakh households a day; 4,000+ waste pickers; 82,891 t recycled a year; ₹113 crore saved for the corporation; over 70% of Pune covered. Programmes include multilayer plastic collection with ITC and the corporation, and a "Recycling Trail" of field visits showing how the city handles its waste. Partnership page: a 2005 pilot trained 1,500 waste pickers; the cooperative formed in 2007; a five-year agreement with the corporation in 2008; over 850 t a day collected, of which about 150 t recycled and 130 t composted; ₹20 crore a year saved. Caveats: advocacy pages, self-reported; fees and members' earnings are not described on the pages opened.
Fit to Satna Partial. The rule fits (the dossier already says: route material to existing collectors, never around them). A city cooperative built on a 30-year union cannot be recreated in 10 weeks.
Phase 2 borrows Map local kabadiwalas and sanitation workers in week 1 and pay them as the channel. Borrow the Recycling Trail as a receiver visit for each women's group.
Does not transfer The scale, the union history and the municipal contract.
Sources https://swachcoop.com/ ; https://swachcoop.com/about/swach-pmc-partnership/ (both accessed 2026-11-16). Also PARTNER_MAP.md F10.

I5 · Hasiru Dala and Bengaluru's dry waste collection centres: door-to-door collection fills centres with low-value plastic
What it is Dry waste collection centres (DWCCs) run by waste pickers with Hasiru Dala's support. Bengaluru's 2020 bye-law requires DWCCs to be run by waste pickers or their self-help groups.
Key strategy Give waste pickers the dry-waste centre, with identity cards and a stable income.
Evidence Citizen Matters (Nalini Shekar, Hasiru Dala co-founder, 20 May 2019): 198 DWCCs sanctioned, 166 working, 0.5–4.5 t a day each; 33 run by waste pickers with Hasiru Dala; after operators began door-to-door collection, low-value waste rose to about 82% of the dry waste collected (from a study with University of Washington students; the method is not given on the page); centres are overrun with bags of multilayer plastic and mattresses. Earlier verified: the 2020 bye-law and a ward DWCC run by a waste-picker entrepreneur with 16 mostly women workers (literature_verified.md #22, accessed 2026-11-15); 10,437 waste pickers given occupational ID cards and 8,781 linked to social security (PARTNER_MAP.md F9, accessed 2026-11-15).
Fit to Satna Strong as a warning. If Phase 2 collects door to door, most of what it collects will be low-value plastic with no buyer.
Phase 2 borrows Decide where low-value plastic goes before collecting it; weigh and report it as its own stream.
Does not transfer A city bye-law, 166 centres and a citywide card scheme.
Sources https://citizenmatters.in/dry-waste-collection-centres-need-design-changes-to-make-them-function-better/ (accessed 2026-11-16); Hasiru Dala case study PDF via literature_verified.md #22 and 30stades via PARTNER_MAP.md F9 (accessed 2026-11-15).

I6 · Mysuru and Saahas Zero Waste: brands' EPR money opened a paid route for multilayer plastic
What it is Mysuru City Corporation runs a decentralised public-private "Zero Waste Management Scheme" with ward-level units. Saahas Zero Waste, funded by brand owners under EPR, built a reverse supply chain for multilayer plastic (MLP) from those units to authorised recycling or co-processing destinations, through a local scrap entrepreneur.
Key strategy Brand EPR purchase orders create a paid route for multilayer plastic, with records at each step.
Evidence Saahas case study (August 2020, self-reported): city of about 1 million, 400–450 t of waste a day; the chain runs from door-to-door collection to ward units, dry-waste centres, aggregation and dispatch; steps include data reconciliation, recycling or co-processing certificates from end destinations, reports to the EPR client and monthly third-party audits; 5,625 t collected from FY 2017-18 to June 2020 (462, 898, 2,527 and 1,738 t by year); the entrepreneur handles 10–12 t of dry waste a day with 26 field staff; the page says long-term purchase orders made collection steady and built trust. The same report converts the tonnage into 16,537 t CO2e "saved" and a seedling equivalent using a US EPA calculator, with no life-cycle method for this material.
Fit to Satna Strong on the material: it is the clearest precedent for sachets and wrappers. Partial on the actors: L&T is not a brand owner with a plastic EPR target, so the route would need an EPR processor or co-processor as the receiver.
Phase 2 borrows Ask an EPR processor or co-processor to receive low-value plastic, and copy its paper chain (dispatch slip, certificate) into the Trust Receipt.
Does not transfer City-scale brand purchase orders; the CO2e and seedling equivalents, which claim beyond the evidence and would fail the Claim Ladder at rung 6.
Sources https://saahaszerowaste.com/explore/reports-and-case-study/lets-transform-reverse-supply-chain-for-plastic-waste-mysuru-case-study.pdf (accessed 2026-11-16; text extracted locally from the PDF). Also PARTNER_MAP.md F8.

I7 · Swachh Bharat Mission (Grameen) Phase II: a plastic unit per block, run by self-help groups or the panchayat
What it is Phase II of the national rural sanitation mission provides for at least one Plastic Waste Management Unit (PWMU) in each block, fed by village segregation sheds, with shredding and baling for sale.
Key strategy Village sheds feed one block-level unit that shreds and bales plastic for sale.
Evidence Uttarakhand SBM-G page (updated 5 Jun 2025): one PWMU per block, up to ₹16 lakh per unit; Phase II approved in February 2020 and run from 2020-21 to 2024-25. Ballia (Uttar Pradesh) district note on the UP Panchayati Raj Department's server (unsigned working note, January 2026): two PWMUs; village sheds and tricycles for door-to-door collection; operation by SHGs or the gram panchayat, paid from sales of shredded or baled plastic and household user charges; monthly checks by the district panchayat officer; links planned to PWD road building and cement kilns. Challenges it lists: moving light plastic from remote villages is costly; household segregation is inconsistent; running costs exceed the value of processed plastic; local kabadiwalas are hard to bring in. The national number of working PWMUs is [UNVERIFIED] (PIB pages returned 403).
Fit to Satna Strong: this is the government system the villages around the site belong to. Whether a PWMU operates in the site's block is [UNVERIFIED].
Phase 2 borrows In week 1, check whether the block's PWMU or a village shed near the site works; if so, deliver baled low-value plastic there against a slip.
Does not transfer It is infrastructure, not a behaviour programme: nothing in it tells a household where its plastic went.
Sources https://swajal.uk.gov.in/?p=6683 ; https://fs.upprd.in/Upload/SBMG_DOCUMENTS/639042682177494047_PWMU%20FRAMEWORK.pdf (both accessed 2026-11-16; the PDF text was extracted locally).

I8 · Rural Madhya Pradesh: built sheds stand idle where nobody is paid to run them
What it is A 101Reporters investigation (10 Jan 2024) across Vidisha, Chhatarpur, Panna, Mandla and rural Bhopal: SBM-G assets, and two working exceptions run by NGOs.
Key strategy Cluster villages and pay sanitation workers, so built assets actually operate.
Evidence Figures quoted by the article: the SBM dashboard lists 16,515 waste collection and segregation centres and 34,989 villages with solid waste management in MP; a CSE scoping paper found 1.18% of 22,814 village panchayats with a plastic waste system and sheds (the CSE paper itself was not opened: [UNVERIFIED] at source). Observed: a recovery facility at Karimabad (Vidisha) set up about three years earlier is shut; a similar one at Vardha has not been used. The state SBM deputy commissioner told the reporter there was no village-level facility for solid and plastic waste, citing a lack of staff, monitoring and awareness. Working exceptions: Sarthak's model covers 222 panchayats in 22 clusters around Bhopal, with sanitation workers collecting about 107 t a day and SHGs making products from plastic and rubber; at Madla (Panna) an NGO collects 40–50 kg of plastic a round and sends it to a cement factory at Amanganj. Also: 405 t of plastic went into 938 km of roads in 2020-21.
Fit to Satna Strong: Satna is in the same region and the same scheme. This is the closest evidence to what Phase 2 will meet.
Phase 2 borrows Treat operations (a paid person, a schedule, a receiver) as the intervention, not the shed.
Does not transfer Sarthak's 222-panchayat scale. The 1.18% figure stays second-hand until the CSE paper is opened.
Sources https://101reporters.com/article/environment/Operational_issues_up_plastic_pollution_in_rural_areas_of_Madhya_Pradesh (accessed 2026-11-16).

I9 · Akshar Forum, Assam: pupils bring household plastic each week and the school turns it into ecobricks
What it is A school in Pamohi, on the outskirts of Guwahati, that takes plastic waste from pupils in place of fees and packs it into ecobricks for building.
Key strategy Children bring household plastic weekly; the school turns it into building material.
Evidence Thomson Reuters Foundation, republished by Eco-Business (13 Jun 2019): pupils bring at least 20 pieces of plastic a week in exchange for free schooling; the plastic becomes ecobricks for construction; parents resisted at first and later sent plastic (no counts). Caveats: based on a phone interview with a co-founder; the plan for older pupils to earn from plastic is stated as an intention; no outcome data.
Fit to Satna Partial. Schools near the site took part in Phase 1, and ecobricks are already EcoCraft's hook. But the dossier forbids data collection from children.
Phase 2 borrows A weekly school "plastic day": children bring clean, dry wrappers from home; the school's batch is weighed as one; a picture card goes home to parents.
Does not transfer Plastic as a fee, and children earning from waste: both tie a child's schooling or income to waste, which the pilot must not do.
Sources https://www.eco-business.com/news/trash-for-teaching-indian-students-hope-to-set-example-on-plastic-recycling (accessed 2026-11-16).

4. Strategy extraction: eight moves for EcoCraft Phase 2

All PLANNED. "From" names the models that supply the mechanism; the Satna adaptation is analysis.

# Strategy From How we adapt it at Satna What we will measure
S1 One fixed weekly handover per group, same place, same person, timed to the group's routine I1, I2, I3, B7 One slot a week per group (colony gate after the shift, the women's group meeting, the school's plastic day), with a call or WhatsApp reminder that morning. 5.2 Pilot design in plain terms

  • Unit of analysis: the group (a colony block or crew, a village women's group). Groups are matched in pairs on size and baseline contamination (dossier §7). School batches are recorded at school level and kept out of the matched comparison, because the dossier forbids data from children.
  • Independent variables:
  • IV1 Trust Receipt (yes / no): H1, H3, H4.
  • IV2 Identity framing vs standard signage: tested cleanly on campus (H2, ABAB swaps).
  • IV3 (optional) delivery format: a 10-minute talk inside a toolbox talk vs a stand-alone session (H5, if the site allows).
  • Dependent variables: primary: repeat participation (returning ÷ total, rolling four weeks) and contamination (% by weight). Secondary: kg per contributor per week (group kg ÷ people present, no names), receiver acceptance (% of delivered kg the receiver accepts, rung 3), cost per kg, sharp items unwrapped, trust (interviews).
  • Moderators: group type, group size, baseline contamination, distance to the handover point, saleable share, literacy, pay-day and festival weeks.
  • Held constant across arms: the collection service, bags, schedule, collector, receiver, Phase 1 sessions; no fees or payments to households.

Consistency with the dossier (H1–H5). The names match: Trust Receipt, Seva Circle, Claim Ladder, repeat participation, contamination by weight. Four points for the consistency pass: 1. The receipt and the Seva Circle are bundled at Satna. The dossier's method gives treatment groups both; H1 attributes the effect to the receipt. As written, Satna tests the bundle. Isolating the receipt needs at least one group with the receipt and no Seva Circle; with four groups that leaves one group per cell, so effects would be descriptive. This file assumes Satna tests the bundle and the campus isolates framing (H2); see the assumptions log. 2. "kg per contributor per week" and "receiver acceptance" are new derived measures. Both come from data the dossier already collects (weekly weights, head-counts, delivery slips). 3. Schools sit outside the comparison, as above. 4. Phase 2 length: the brief says 8–10 weeks; the dossier's comparison is 8 weeks. The extra weeks are read as set-up and baseline.

6.2 What is different about this approach (stated honestly)

  1. Feedback to contributors. Of the 19 models reviewed, none stated that it tells contributors where their own batch went. Five do part of it: Plastic Bank shows its log to funders, Seoul shows households their own weight, SWaCH runs city tours, the ecobrick standard logs each brick, and Eco-Schools reports a school's own progress. Phase 2's Trust Receipt points the batch record back at the people who handed the waste over.
  2. Claims that stop where the evidence stops. None of the 19 pages states a rule that public claims stop at the last evidenced rung. Four go part of the way (Mysuru's audits and certificates, the ecobrick standard, Eco-Schools' measured impact, Seoul's open discussion of attribution). Mysuru's own report shows why a rule is needed: it converts tonnes into CO2e and seedlings without a life-cycle method. The Claim Ladder applies the rule to every receipt and slide.
  3. A worksite as a temporary host. None of the 19 is hosted by an infrastructure contractor's site for the villages around it; the hosts are cities, panchayats, cooperatives, NGOs, schools, a national mission and independent organisations. Phase 2 would test whether a temporary site can start a service that a women's group or panchayat continues after the contractor leaves. This depends on L&T agreeing, which is not yet recorded.
  4. Making as the hook, with a route beyond the object. Three of the 19 use making as the entry hook (Akshar Forum, the ecobrick standard, bottle schools), and all three end at the made object. Phase 2 uses the ecobrick as the hook and the proof, then sends surplus plastic to a named receiver and labels bricks as storage.
  5. Cultural framing as a choice. Faith or ritual appears in three models (Indore's religious leaders, Umuganda, soji). None of those pages describes offering a secular frame beside it; Umuganda is compulsory. Phase 2 lets each group choose a seva frame or a secular one.
  6. What is not new. A regular service and women or waste workers as paid operators are well established: 11 of the 19 run a regular service, and four of the nine Indian models are operated by women's groups or waste workers. Phase 2 borrows these. What is different is the combination: no reviewed model has more than three of the eight features, and none has F4–F7.

These statements cover the 19 models and the pages opened for them, not every programme that exists.
